# Reinforcement Learning for LLM Reasoning: Algorithms, Rewards, Efficiency and Unsettled Debates

## TL;DR
- The field has converged on critic-free, group-relative policy optimization driven by verifiable rewards: the lineage runs from PPO's clipped surrogate objective [1] through RLHF with a per-token KL penalty against the SFT policy [2] and DPO's RL-free preference objective [3] to GRPO's group-score baseline with KL moved into the loss [4], the recipe used by large reasoning models such as DeepSeek-R1 [5].
- Reward design, not optimizer choice, carries most of the reported gains, but every reward channel has a documented failure mode: rule-based verifiers are preferred over neural reward models because of reward hacking [5], while process reward models (PRMs) themselves can be driven to near-perfect reward with almost no ground-truth accuracy [6].
- Data-side interventions are among the cheapest gains: filtering for problems of intermediate difficulty adds roughly 10 points on AIME over plain GRPO and reaches GRPO's peak reward in 60% of the training time [7], and staged response-length curricula underpin large math/code gains for 7B-14B models [8].
- The central scientific dispute is unresolved: RL is argued to sharpen the base model's sampling distribution (pass@1 up, pass@256 coverage down) [9], yet prolonged RL is reported to beat base models across pass@k [10]; recent work reframes the gains as low-dimensional activation steering rather than new capability [11].
- Efficiency and new settings are moving faster than the debate: async on-policy pipelines report roughly 2x faster learning on 128 H100s [12], and executable-environment agentic RL reports gains with only 85 environments [13], while evaluation integrity is becoming a first-class concern [14][15].

## Background
Reinforcement learning for LLM reasoning means post-training a language model with a reward signal -- typically the correctness of a final answer -- so that it emits long chains of thought and solves math, code, or tool-use tasks. The base optimizer is PPO, which permits multiple epochs of minibatch updates on a clipped surrogate objective [1]. The first widely used pipeline was InstructGPT-style RLHF: supervised fine-tuning, then a reward model, then PPO against that model with a per-token KL penalty from the SFT model to limit over-optimization [2]. DPO then showed the same KL-constrained objective can be optimized by a classification loss over preferences, eliminating sampling from the language model during fine-tuning [3]. The reasoning-specific turn came with GRPO, which removes the critic and estimates the baseline from the average reward of a group of sampled answers to the same question, regularizing via a KL term added to the loss rather than to the reward [4]. This matters now because the retrieved foundational work from 2022-2023 targets alignment and instruction following rather than long chain-of-thought reasoning, and the transition to reasoning was driven by separate 2024-2026 systems papers rather than stated in any single retrieved source.

## Theme 1: The algorithmic lineage from PPO to group-relative methods
The four algorithms differ mainly in what they use as a baseline and where they place the divergence penalty. PPO keeps a learned value function and constrains updates with a clipped probability ratio [1]; InstructGPT initializes that value function from the reward model and adds a per-token KL penalty from the SFT model at every token [2]. DPO removes sampling entirely by parameterizing the optimal policy of the KL-constrained reward-maximization problem, so the policy itself encodes an implicit reward `beta * log pi(y|x)/pi_ref(y|x)`; the authors report that DPO exceeds PPO-based RLHF at controlling sentiment on their evaluations, while flagging open questions about out-of-distribution generalization and reward over-optimization [3]. GRPO is the reasoning-era compromise: it "foregoes the critic model, instead estimating the baseline from group scores" of multiple samples to the same question, and adds the KL divergence directly to the loss instead of the reward, with reported GSM8K gains from 82.9 to 88.2 and MATH from 46.8 to 51.7 [4]. The 2026 literature increasingly problematizes precisely the objective that made GRPO popular: because the final answer is verified only at the end of the trace, the reward is delayed, and GRPO-style methods are characterized as "Monte Carlo methods in standard RL, which are known to suffer from high variance", motivating segment-level reward redistribution [16]. A parallel strand replaces KL control with entropy-based gradient clipping rather than KL penalties, as reported for long-context training [17]. Caveats worth carrying forward: the PPO paper predates language-model reward modelling entirely [1], DPO's reasoning evidence is indirect and its evaluation stops well below frontier scale [3], and GRPO's advantage normalization is only partially documented in the retrieved text [4].

## Theme 2: Verifiable rewards, data curation and RL compute scaling
The now-standard RLVR recipe is a rule-based accuracy reward plus a format reward, deliberately avoiding learned reward models. DeepSeek-R1 checks boxed final answers (with code judged by a compiler) and enforces thinking tags, combining both rewards with equal weight, and the authors state they "abstain from applying neural reward models ... to reasoning tasks" because such models are susceptible to reward hacking; training mixes GRPO with an iterative loop in which new SFT data is created by rejection sampling on RL checkpoints, and reports an AIME 2024 pass@1 trajectory from 15.6% to 71.0% for its RL-trained reasoning models [5]. Scale and curriculum matter as much as the objective: AceReason-Nemotron sequences math-only RL before code-only RL with a progressively extended response-length budget (8K to 32K) and reports +14.6%/+17.2% on AIME 2025 for 7B/14B models and +6.8%/+5.8% on LiveCodeBench [8]. Data selection has a measurable effect: online difficulty filtering, which curates each batch toward problems the current model solves with intermediate accuracy, gives "an additional 10% in AIME and 4% improvements in average over plain GRPO" and reaches plain GRPO's maximum reward within 60% of the training time and data volume [7]. The objective itself is biased in at least one axis: a critical re-examination of R1-Zero-style training identifies "an optimization bias in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), which artificially increases response length (especially for incorrect outputs)", and proposes Dr. GRPO as an unbiased alternative that improves token efficiency, with a minimal 7B recipe reaching 43.3% on AIME 2024 [18]. On scaling, ProRL reports that "prolonged" RL "can uncover novel reasoning strategies that are inaccessible to base models, even under extensive sampling" and that reasoning-boundary improvement "correlates strongly with task competence of base model and training duration", using KL control, reference-policy resetting and a diverse task suite [10]. Two gaps are important: no retrieved source performs a compute-matched head-to-head comparison of learned reward models against rule-based verifiable rewards, and the difficulty-filtering evidence is restricted to math benchmarks [7].

## Theme 3: Process, outcome and implicit rewards
Step-level supervision was the first response to sparse final-answer rewards. Let's Verify Step by Step reports that "process supervision significantly outperforms outcome supervision for training models to solve problems from the challenging MATH dataset", with a process-supervised PRM solving 78.2% of a representative MATH subset, at the price of collecting 1,085,590 step-level human labels over 101,599 solution samples [19]. Math-Shepherd attacks that cost by generating step labels automatically from rollout completions and using the resulting PRM in two ways -- reranking candidate solutions and step-level PPO -- reporting Mistral-7B improvements from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH, while noting that human annotation "can be quite costly, which hinders the advancement and practical application of PRM" [20]. The 2025-2026 wave pushes further toward implicit rewards: PRIME "enables online PRM updates using only policy rollouts and outcome labels through implicit process rewards", forgoing a dedicated reward-model training phase and reporting a 15.1% average improvement over the SFT model from a Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Base start, with the resulting 7B model surpassing Qwen2.5-Math-7B-Instruct on seven reasoning benchmarks using 10% of its training data [21]. LaSeR supplies a theoretical anchor, arguing that the closed-form solution to the self-verification RL objective reduces to the last-token self-rewarding score and aligning that score with verifier rewards via an MSE term at the cost of one additional token of inference [22]; this builds on the earlier self-rewarding paradigm in which a model generates and judges its own preferences with LLM-as-a-judge iterative DPO [23]. The countervailing evidence is strong: an adversarial study finds policies that "achieve near-perfect PRM rewards (> 0.9), while ground-truth accuracy remains low (below 4%), with 43% of reward gains attributable to stylistic shortcuts", concluding that "current PRMs function as fluency detectors rather than reasoning verifiers" [6]. A mechanistic account complements this: RLVR-induced capacity appears to lie on a "low-dimensional effective manifold in activation space" that is "very small but is not infinitely compressible", the learned geometry stays "largely consistent across training configurations" within a given task and base model, and cross-task geometric alignment correlates with capability transfer across five LLMs and six verifiable-reward tasks [11].

## Theme 4: Does RL expand reasoning capability, or reweight it?
This is the sharpest disagreement in the literature and it should not be presented as settled. The critical position is that "all reasoning paths in the RLVR model are already present in the base model", so RLVR "biases the distribution toward rewarded paths, improving sampling efficiency" at the "cost of reduced scope of reasoning capacity"; the supporting pattern is that as training progresses pass@1 improves while pass@256 coverage of solvable problems falls [9]. The opposing evidence comes from prolonged training, where RL-trained models are reported to outperform base models across pass@k and to uncover strategies inaccessible to the base model even under extensive sampling [10]. The mechanistic work offers a possible reconciliation, showing that the RLVR change is compressible into a small number of activation directions that stay stable across training configurations [11]. Meanwhile, the algorithmic critique suggests part of the measured "improvement" is an artifact of bias in the estimator: GRPO's length bias inflates response length, especially on incorrect outputs [18], and the delayed-reward formulation implies high-variance advantage estimates [16]. Because the two sides differ in training budget, model scale and evaluation protocol, the retrieved evidence supports describing the capability-boundary question as open and method-dependent rather than resolved in either direction.

## Theme 5: Efficiency, infrastructure and the on-policyness trade-off
Infrastructure work converges on the same bottleneck: generation, not gradient computation. A vendor engineering report states that in PPO-style RLHF "generating long chain-of-thought (CoT) outputs can account for up to 90% of total training time", and describes OpenRLHF using Ray placement groups to schedule the vLLM engine, actor, critic, reference and reward models into shared placement groups with weight synchronization over NCCL or CUDA IPC [24]. The colocation approach is complementary rather than identical: TRL moved vLLM from a separate-server design, which caused "idle time and underutilization", into the same process group, relying, as that blog reports, on vLLM's `sleep()` API to release weights and KV cache inside the GRPO loop plus DeepSpeed ZeRO-3 with CPU optimizer offload, and reporting that "even with 4 fewer GPUs, the co-locate setup is ~1.26x faster than plain TRL" on Qwen2.5-Math-72B [25]. Pipelining buys more but at a cost to on-policyness: PipelineRL performs "concurrent asynchronous data generation and model training ... in-flight weight updates" and reports approximately 2x faster learning on 128 H100s while keeping training "highly on-policy", using online sequence packing and ring buffers to bound lag, with stale-KV-cache risk mitigated by recomputation [12]. Long-context training adds a different kind of efficiency pressure: a 30B reasoning model is extended to work over 256K-token contexts, with the recipe leaning "on GRPO rather than PPO to avoid value-network overhead", replacing KL penalties with entropy-statistics-based gradient clipping (AEPO), training strictly on-policy with one update per batch, and using token-normalized objectives so long answers do not dominate [17]. The common thread is that these systems trade memory scheduling, staleness and discount/regularizer design against on-policyness; the retrieved infrastructure evidence, however, is dominated by self-reported vendor and model-owner engineering blogs rather than independent measurement [24][25].

## Theme 6: Agentic and long-context RL, and evaluation integrity
The newest frontier extends the same critic-free, verifiable-reward machinery to multi-turn tool use and long context. EnvFactory identifies two bottlenecks for agentic RL -- "the lack of scalable, robust execution environments and the scarcity of realistic training data" -- and reports that 85 verified environments across 7 domains suffice to synthesize 2,575 SFT and RL trajectories, improving Qwen3-series models by up to +15% on BFCLv3, +8.6% on MCP-Atlas and +6% on conversational benchmarks including tau^2-Bench and VitaBench, compared with baselines using five times more environments [13]; the same long-context/post-training line of work pairs 256K-token RL with a memory-augmented agent for 1M-4M token documents, distilling roughly 14k RL samples from about 42k candidates over ~82k long documents [17]. Alongside this expansion comes evidence that reported gains are fragile to evaluation design. Training-time contamination is argued to be decisive: pre-training on web-scale corpora leaves Qwen2.5 "susceptible to data contamination in widely used benchmarks", so benchmark-derived conclusions about that family "may be unreliable", and on a synthetic leakage-free arithmetic generator "only accurate reward signals yield steady improvements ... whereas random or incorrect rewards do not" [14]. Symbolic variabilization of contest benchmarks produces average declines of 47.9% on AMC23, 58.8% on AIME24 and 72.9% on AIME25 for RL-trained models [15]. Detection is being formalized: after the RL phase the output entropy distribution "tends to collapse into highly specific and sparse modes", and a Self-Critique detector on the RL-MIA benchmark reports "an AUC improvement of up to 30%" while existing methods are "close to a random guess for RL-phase contamination" [26]. Taken together, these results mean that agentic and reasoning gains should be read against leakage-aware and variabilized evaluations, not only standard leaderboards.

## Trends and open problems
Three trends are visible across the last two years. First, methodological convergence around GRPO-style, critic-free optimization with rule-based verifiable rewards [4][5], followed immediately by a wave of objective-level corrections: length bias and unbiased alternatives [18], delayed-reward credit assignment [16], and entropy-controlled stabilization instead of KL penalties [17]. Second, the research frontier moving from single-turn math RL to long-context and agentic settings with synthesized executable environments [13][17], which makes reward design and credit assignment harder rather than easier. Third, evaluation integrity becoming part of the method itself, with synthetic leakage-free generators [14], variabilized benchmarks [15] and RL-specific contamination detectors [26].

The open problems, as evidenced rather than asserted: the capability-boundary dispute between reweighting [9] and expansion [10] is unresolved and confounded by training budget and protocol differences; PRM hackability [6] undercuts the step-level supervision that the foundational PRM line [19][20] argued for, while implicit-reward methods [21][22] propose a way out but are evidenced here only at abstract level; the efficiency literature's central claims come largely from vendor blogs rather than independent replication [24][25]; difficulty filtering is demonstrated on math only [7]; and agentic multi-turn credit assignment, memory profiling and cost accounting are not established by the retrieved sources [13]. A notable meta-finding of this survey is that no single retrieved source documents the transition from 2022-2023 alignment-era RLHF to 2024-2026 reasoning-era RLVR, so that narrative here is synthesized from separate papers [1][2][3][4].

Verification limitations: all 26 retained sources were checked for existence and metadata, and every URL resolved; however, for two Hugging Face pages the rendered publication date is one day earlier than the structured metadata used here [11][13]. Some fine-grained details could not be independently re-verified within the retrieval budget: the specific reward-weighting and AIME figures attributed to DeepSeek-R1 were only reachable through the paper's abstract and introduction [5], and the co-location mechanics and speedup figure reported for TRL [25] sit below the fetched portion of that blog. The 2017-2025 numeric results quoted in Themes 1-3 are reported by their authors on their own benchmarks, not independently replicated by this survey.
